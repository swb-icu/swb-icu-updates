Research Corner
Trials • Recruitment • Airway Registry

A compact research section this month: a well-earned SHORTER ranking, September's screening and recruitment numbers, and a new national registry for emergency front-of-neck airway cases.

🏆 SHORTER Trial: Second Nationally We received some well-deserved recognition this month for our outstanding contribution to recruitment in the SHORTER Trial. Our Trust ranked second nationally, just one participant behind our neighbours at University Hospitals Birmingham (UHB). A fantastic achievement for the team, and thank you to everyone who keeps screening and flagging patients.

September at a Glance

In September, 42 patients were screened and 18 were recruited into research on the unit. Thank you to everyone involved in screening, consenting and looking after our research patients.

New: eFONAr, the Emergency Front of Neck Airway Registry

🩹 Had a Plan D? Please Tell Us Our Trust is participating in the eFONA Registry, a collaborative project between the Difficult Airway Society (DAS) and the Royal College of Anaesthetists Centre for Research and Improvement. The registry follows on from the work of NAP4 and has been collecting data on eFONA cases since 30 March 2026.

If you encounter an eFONA (emergency front-of-neck airway) case, please let Bharati, Wasim or Preksha know.

Cases for inclusion:
  • All cases in which an eFONA procedure has been attempted or performed, whether successful or unsuccessful
  • Patients of all ages
  • Non-emergency front-of-neck access procedures should not be entered
The eFONAr poster. Scan the QR code on it for more details about the registry. See also the difficult airway trolley item under Clinical Updates.
🏫 eFONAr on the Difficult Airway Society website
Background on the registry, a joint project of DAS, the Royal College of Anaesthetists and the University of Nottingham, open for cases from 30 March 2026.

Clinical Updates
Protocols • Processes • Equipment

Three practical updates this month: a change to who attends Cath Lab emergencies, a winter reminder on respiratory swabs, and a piece of airway equipment that is easy to forget until you need it.

🚧 EMRT Process Change: Cath Lab Emergencies An update has been made to the Emergency Medical Response Team (EMRT) process to provide enhanced critical care support, and it is already in place.

Cath Lab emergencies: the ICU Registrar now attends all Cath Lab EMRT calls alongside the standard EMRT team, providing additional senior-tier airway and critical care support. Bleeps go out on both 6139 and 5947.

Registrar coordination: ICU registrars should coordinate amongst themselves to make sure someone attends every Cath Lab call, or use the usual escalation arrangements if engaged in a time-critical activity.

All other hospital areas: no change to the standard EMRT response for non-Cath Lab calls.
🦠 Winter Reminder: Respiratory Swabs and Isolation Please ensure that all patients admitted with respiratory symptoms have a swab taken and sent for testing. To reduce the risk of transmission and prevent contacts, any patient awaiting a COVID or flu swab result must be isolated until their result is available.

Important reminder about swab samples: please order and use the swabs and the purple sample bags, and request COVID, Influenza and RSV PCR on Unity.
🩺 Difficult Airway Trolley: Biopsy Forceps for Central Airway Obstruction A small number of emergencies in our ICU have involved a central airway obstruction caused by a large blood clot or an immovable clump of secretions. The Plan A section of the Difficult Airway Trolley includes biopsy forceps for these situations, and they will fit through the Ambuscope.

Hopefully they will rarely be needed, but they are on the stock checklist for the ICU trolleys. They are not part of the other Difficult Airway Trolleys across the Trust, so it is worth knowing they are here before you ever need them.

Training & Standards
PPE • Appearance • Mandatory Training

This month's training and standards section is about protecting yourself and presenting yourself well, which turn out to be closely linked.

😷 PPE When Intubating: Infectious Status Is Often Unknown We do not always know the infectious status of patients at the point of intubation, particularly in an emergency in ED or with EMRT. To protect yourself from exposure, you may choose to wear PPE (FFP3 mask and visor) for this procedure.
👔 Uniform and Appearance: Professional, Identifiable, Leading by Example How we look helps patients and relatives know who we are, supports infection control, and shows the standard we hold ourselves to. A quick refresher:
  • ICU uniform only when on duty - no theatre scrubs
  • Yellow name badge visible at all times, so patients, relatives and colleagues can see your name and role. No lanyards
  • Hand hygiene before and after every patient contact; bare below the elbows in clinical areas
  • No long, synthetic or varnished nails
  • Hair collar length or above
  • Minimal jewellery: one plain ring and one pair of plain studs
  • Cardigans and jackets removed in clinical areas
🤝 Lead by Example, and It Is Fine to Ask Whatever your role or seniority, people notice what we do, so please lead by example. If you are unsure whether something meets the standard, ask. And if you notice a colleague's badge or appearance slipping, it is perfectly okay to say so, kindly and quietly. A word between colleagues is far better than a missed infection control point.
✅ Mandatory Training Please check that your mandatory training is current. If anything has lapsed, complete it promptly.

Safety & Quality
Learning • Governance • Legal Know-How

A heavy but valuable month for learning. Dr Ahmed Mokhtar has shared five key messages from recent incident reviews and conversations, summarised below and then in more detail. There is also an update from our NMP lead, and a new monthly feature: a Midnight Law.

Learning from Incidents

The September 2026 Learning from Incidents board. Key takeaways include agreeing and documenting an MDT dosing and monitoring plan for high-risk anticoagulation, documenting a clinical examination on the day of discharge, and reviewing fluid balance before extubation.
The September 2026 board, with 58 incidents entered. Key takeaways include property checks on admission and every 7 days, checking observations within an hour of ward discharge, and completing wound charts for all skin damage.
💧 Fluid Balance Assessment Before Extubation A recent review of a failed extubation highlighted significant positive fluid balance as a potential contributing factor, alongside VAP and airway oedema. The extubation checklist was completed, but cumulative fluid balance had not been highlighted during the assessment. The learning reinforces the importance of reviewing cumulative fluid balance and clinical volume status, particularly after prolonged ventilation, and considering fluid optimisation where appropriate to reduce the risk of pulmonary oedema and early extubation failure.

Key message: before extubation, review cumulative fluid balance and clinical volume status, and consider whether further fluid optimisation is needed.
💉 Therapeutic LMWH in High-Risk Patients A recent multidisciplinary incident review highlighted the importance of individual risk-benefit assessments when considering therapeutic LMWH in high-risk patients, particularly those with severe AKI requiring CRRT. Key learning includes documenting the assessment, seeking multidisciplinary input, considering alternative anticoagulants, and agreeing clear dosing and monitoring plans. Work is ongoing with Haematology to finalise guidance on anti-Xa monitoring and interpretation.

Key message: in high-risk patients, document the risk-benefit assessment and agree the anticoagulant, dose and monitoring plan with multidisciplinary input.
📋 Safe Handover and Continuity of Care Complete discharge or transfer documentation supports safe handover when patients leave ITU. The medical team is responsible for completing the note, with nursing leaders, including zone leaders and shift coordinators, providing an essential additional safety check that it is available before transfer. Missing documentation should be escalated promptly. A review has been requested with nursing leadership to strengthen these shared checks and support a consistent, reliable discharge process.

Key message: before transfer, confirm the discharge or transfer note is complete and available to the receiving team.
🌙 Keeping Care Plans on Track Out of Hours Making sure morning ward round plans are delivered through the day and overnight is a shared responsibility across the medical and nursing teams. Nursing handover is an important safety checkpoint to highlight goals, including fluid balance targets and planned electrolyte correction, and to review progress. Where targets are not being met or treatment is delayed, escalate promptly to the medical team and nurse in charge, and document the agreed response. Timely review and action support continuity of care and help prevent avoidable complications and prolonged ITU stays.

Key message: highlight goals at handover, review progress, and escalate and document when plans are off track.
💕 Supporting Families Through Critical Illness Helping families understand what to expect is an essential part of the psychosocial care of critically ill patients. While our aim is recovery and discharge from ITU, families should understand from the outset that recovery may be prolonged and that their relative may deteriorate or not survive despite treatment. This matters most early in an admission and for patients needing multiorgan support. Positive progress should be shared, while making clear that improvement does not necessarily mean the risk has passed. Timely, compassionate updates, with opportunities to ask questions and share the patient's wishes and values, help families stay informed and involved.

Key message: offer hope alongside honest expectations, and keep families informed and involved throughout the ITU stay.

New Each Month: A Midnight Law

From this issue we will feature one of the CICM (formerly FICM) Midnight Laws each month, chosen to relate to something in that issue. These are one-page summaries of thorny legal issues, designed to be read whenever and wherever a question arises. This month it links to our conversations with families and the care of patients who lack capacity.

⚖️ Midnight Law: Mental Capacity Act and Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS)
The summary covers a Court of Appeal judgment on DoLS in intensive care and notes that implementation of the Liberty Protection Safeguards has been deferred. Read the one-page summary before you need it.
