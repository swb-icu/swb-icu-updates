Clinical Updates
Protocols • Guidelines • Equipment
Pupillometer SOP - Now Live on SPARC The Pupillometer SOP is now live on SPARC. The pupillometer should be used for any patient falling into an acquired brain injury category:
  • Traumatic: head injuries - penetrating or non-penetrating
  • Non-traumatic: hypoxia/anoxia, stroke, infection/disease, brain tumours, poisoning
In practice, that means: all patients with pupillary changes on initial neurological assessment; all patients with a suspected acquired brain injury awaiting a CT scan; all patients with a diagnosed acquired brain injury on CT scan; and patients on the perceived devastating brain injury pathway. Please do not use the pupillometer for general neurological assessment on non-acquired-brain-injury patients - use a pen torch instead. If there's suspicion of a head injury in any other patient, discuss use of the pupillometer with the medical team or shift coordinator. Read the full SOP →
Russell PneumoFix - Chest Decompression Device The Russell PneumoFix is a sterile chest decompression device for the management of tension pneumothorax - a catheter device with a low-pressure release valve, inserted over a Veress-tipped needle. These are stocked in the ICU emergency trolley and should be used in preference to a Venflon for this purpose. If you're someone who may need to decompress a tension pneumothorax, please familiarise yourself with how it works - we're also organising rolling hands-on training sessions. Extubation Checklist & Discharge Documentation Please ensure the Extubation Checklist is completed for all patients being considered for extubation. Laminated checklists are available in each ICU zone - please use these as part of the assessment. A Unity form must also be completed by a member of the medical team prior to extubation (see your email for how, or ask an ACCP). Full guidance on documenting spontaneous breathing trials and assessing extubation readiness was covered in last month's issue and remains current.
Reminder: ICU Discharge & Transfer Documentation For all patient transfers - including step-down to the wards and transfers to other hospitals - please complete an ICU Discharge Summary in ICU Workflow, including the name of the person you handed the patient over to. This applies to time-critical transfers too, where possible. Please also complete a Discharge Summary (GP Letter) in the 'Depart' tab for all patients discharged from ICU, including those discharged home (rare), and patients who self-discharge, abscond, or are discharged against medical advice (DAMA). There's a Shift 6 guide for DAMA patients that outlines everything needed.
📋 Did You Know? - CVC and Cannula Care Central venous catheters should not be routinely changed at a fixed number of days after insertion - there's no evidence this reduces infection risk, and it adds unnecessary line changes and complications. Two things do matter: take it out as soon as it's no longer needed (daily review of line necessity is the single most effective intervention), and take it out if there's reasonable clinical concern it's infected, without waiting for confirmatory culture if suspicion is strong. This isn't new thinking - Peter Pronovost's Keystone ICU project (Michigan, published NEJM 2006) showed a simple checklist (hand hygiene, chlorhexidine skin prep, full barrier precautions, avoiding the femoral site, and daily assessment of catheter necessity) cut CRBSI rates dramatically across over 100 ICUs, with the effect sustained for years. It worked not because any single step was novel, but because it forced the "is this line still needed" question to be asked every day. The same logic applies to peripheral cannulae - no need for routine removal at a fixed day count either; current guidance supports removal based on clinical indication (phlebitis, infiltration, no longer needed) rather than a calendar rule.

⚠️ Keep Bedspace Vents Clear Please ensure the vents in the bedspaces are kept clear of any obstruction. If these vents are blocked, it affects the air flow and reduces the quality of air changes in the room.
Keep bedspace vents clear - blocked vents reduce air flow and air change quality.
Contact Lenses on Admission There has been an incident where a patient had contact lenses in at the time of collapse, and this wasn't recognised on admission - there are now complications to his eye potentially as a result. Please check whether a patient wears contact lenses, or has them in situ, on admission. They should be removed as soon as possible and documented. If there are any concerns regarding eyes or pupil reactions, escalate to the medical team and seek specialist opinion from ophthalmology if necessary.
PPE - Check Infectious Status Always check a patient's infectious status and take precautions to protect yourself and your colleagues. If a patient has respiratory symptoms, it's safer to wear a higher level of PPE - an FFP3 mask - until infection is confirmed or excluded.

Patient Safety • Governance • Audit

Patient Safety Spotlight

Out-of-Hours Working: Early Escalation and Senior Support Out-of-hours working can be challenging, with changing clinical priorities, competing demands and situations that may evolve quickly. Complex clinical decisions, deteriorating patients or difficult conversations with families may all require additional senior input. Maintaining a low threshold for senior discussion and consultant involvement is an important part of safe out-of-hours working - early communication and escalation allow concerns to be shared, support to be coordinated and difficult decisions to be approached as a team. Key message: escalate early, communicate clearly, and seek senior support when needed.
⚠️ Learning from Incidents: Aminoglycosides - Prescribe with Care A recent incident highlighted an important reminder around the safe use of gentamicin, particularly in patients with impaired renal function. Gentamicin should not be prescribed as a routine regular medication and left active on the drug chart - each subsequent dose should be a deliberate clinical decision, guided by the indication, renal function and, where appropriate, gentamicin levels. Risk can increase when usual safeguards are reduced, particularly out of hours or at weekends when pharmacy input may be limited, so careful medication review at every handover and ward round is essential.

Governance & Audit

Every Incident Is an Opportunity to Learn Incidents are reported when a patient safety concern is identified, including near misses. While some identify harm or areas where care could have been different, an incident does not automatically mean a mistake has been made - sometimes there's no clear harm or error, but still valuable learning. As part of a review, you may be contacted by the Governance Team for further information or reflection. Reflection helps us understand what happened, recognise what went well, and consider what we could do differently. Please feel encouraged to share your perspective openly - those directly involved often provide the most valuable insight. The learning may be about an individual action, but is often about communication, teamwork, human factors or the systems around us. Our focus is to learn, improve and make care safer - not to attribute blame.
Audit Recommendations: Discharge Checklist & TEP Forms The ICU Nursing Discharge Checklist should be completed within one hour prior to discharge to the ward. If there's a delay of more than two hours between completing the checklist and the patient's transfer, the checklist must be repeated immediately prior to discharge, so any clinical deterioration or change in condition is identified and managed before transfer. On governance: to prevent ambiguity in interpretation of the TEP, all boxes should be filled in to clearly state any limitations. TEP form contents must be reviewed and signed on the day of discharge for all patients.
